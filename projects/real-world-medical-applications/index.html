Selected research program

AI for Real-World Health Data

Different health data regimes fail in different ways. I start with sparsity, irregularity, noise, scale, and selection, then select methods and interpret results in light of those conditions. Each example is grouped by the data constraint that most strongly shapes its design.

Real-world health data regimes
01Sparse rare-disease dataFew cases, irregular follow-up, and heterogeneous cohorts. 02Large clinical textMany notes, few labels, and institution-specific language. 03High-volume online dataAbundant observations with noise, drift, and selection bias.
01 ·Sparse data

Sparse and irregular data in rare disease

Rare-disease datasets contain few confirmed cases, irregular observations, missing follow-up, and variation across clinics, cohorts, and trials. I preserve longitudinal structure, combine compatible sources, and calibrate augmentation or simulation to observed disease trajectories. Synthetic data can expand stress tests and comparators, while patient-level conclusions still require external validation with observed data.

02 ·Clinical text

Large clinical text with limited labels

Large clinical-note collections contain copied text, irrelevant context, inconsistent terminology, and few adjudicated labels. I use domain filters and concept normalization, then compare rule-based, classical, and transformer models against clinician-reviewed labels. Evaluation reports patient-level sensitivity and failure cases because performance can shift across institutions and documentation practices.

03 ·Online health data

High-volume data with noise and selection

High-volume online health data contain informal language, duplicated content, platform changes, and selection bias. I normalize lay terms to clinical concepts, model time explicitly, and stratify signals by relevant populations and behaviors. These analyses can reveal platform-level and population signals, while prevalence estimates and causal claims require representative designs and external evidence.
